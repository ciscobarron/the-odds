Methodology & Sources

How the odds are calculated.

This tool models the structural sorting system of the United States using a chain of conditional probability multipliers — each one derived from peer-reviewed research on the relationship between demographic starting conditions and life outcomes. The evidence base draws from three tiers: administrative and government data (descriptive baselines), top-tier academic research using quasi-experimental and natural experiment designs (causal estimates), and foundational sociological theory (mechanisms). Where sources are descriptive rather than causal, this is noted. The model also applies three empirically-grounded interaction effects where variables are known to moderate each other rather than compound independently — these appear as separate rows in the chain. The model does not claim to be a complete account of any individual life. It claims to make visible what the data consistently show: that starting conditions are among the strongest predictors of where people land, and that those conditions are not equally shared.

The model
Structural multiplier approach
Each demographic input is assigned a multiplier based on the relative probability of achieving above-median educational and economic outcomes given that starting condition. Multipliers compound across inputs. The result is a structural difficulty score that is then converted to a percentile using a log scale anchored at observed minimum and maximum values across the input space. The birth cohort size is provided as context but is not part of the structural score.
Percentile calibration
The 50th percentile is anchored at a structural multiplier of approximately 6 — representing a white or Asian-American male born to one immigrant parent with a high school diploma, in a working-class neighborhood with some economic instability, who completed a bachelor's degree with limited institutional guidance. This approximates the median structural profile of the US adult population given observed demographic distributions.
Interaction effects
Why variables interact rather than simply compound
The model applies three empirically-grounded interaction adjustments where the research shows variables moderate each other rather than operating independently. These appear as separate rows in the chain display, styled distinctly from the base multipliers.
Interaction 1 — Affluent neighborhood dampens parental education penalty (× 0.65)
School quality as partial substitute for parental educational capital
Applied when: neighborhood is middle-class or affluent AND parental education is high school diploma or less.

Wodtke, G.T., Harding, D.J., & Elwert, F. (2011), "Neighborhood Effects in Temporal Perspective," American Sociological Review, 76(5), 713–736. [growing up in the most disadvantaged neighborhood lowers high school graduation by 20 percentage points — implies affluent neighborhoods provide substantial independent educational lift] — Chetty, R. & Friedman, J. (2011), cited in Chetty & Hendren (2018): 40% of the correlation between parental earnings and children's future earnings is attributable to school quality differences. This means school quality — provided by affluent neighborhoods independently of parental education — substitutes for approximately 40% of the parental education effect.
Interaction 2 — Race moderates parental education advantage for Black Americans (× 1.4)
Diminished return on parental educational capital due to persistent discrimination
Applied when: race = Black AND parental education is bachelor's degree or higher.

Assari, S. & Mistry, R. (2018), "Educational Attainment and Intergenerational Mobility; Role of Race and Gender," Behavioral Sciences, 8(11), 107. [significant interaction between race and parental education: parental education produces smaller intergenerational mobility gains for African Americans than for white Americans] — Mechanism: high social status increases contact with white-dominated institutions, which increases exposure to discrimination. As a result, returns to parental educational attainment are systematically smaller for Black Americans. Chetty et al. (2024, "Changing Opportunity"): racial convergence in mobility occurred largely through improved chances for Black children to escape poverty, not through increased access to the top of the income distribution — confirming that upper-end advantages are differentially accessible by race.
Interaction 3 — Low-income neighborhood compounds with low parental education via peer community effects (× 1.2)
Social network homophily amplifies co-occurring disadvantages
Applied when: neighborhood is low-income or frequent moves AND parental education is less than high school.

Chetty, R., Friedman, J., et al. (2024), "Changing Opportunity: Sociological Mechanisms Underlying Growing Class and Shrinking Race Gaps in Economic Mobility," Opportunity Insights Working Paper. [children's outcomes most strongly associated with parental employment rates of peers in same race and class group; effects decay sharply for adjacent cohorts, consistent with social network homophily — low-income children in low-parental-education households face compounded peer community effects beyond simple resource addition] — Crowder, K. & South, S. (2003): for Black adolescents from single-parent households and white adolescents from low-income households, the association between neighborhood disadvantage and dropout risk is particularly strong, consistent with compounding rather than additive effects.

Neighborhood multipliers
Place-based causal effects on mobility and opportunity — strongest causal domain in the model
Causal: Chetty, R. & Hendren, N. (2018), "The Impacts of Neighborhoods on Intergenerational Mobility I & II," Quarterly Journal of Economics, 133(3). [quasi-experimental; childhood exposure effects on adult income causally estimated] — Chetty, R., Hendren, N., & Katz, L. (2016), "The Effects of Exposure to Better Neighborhoods on Children," American Economic Review. [Moving to Opportunity RCT; randomized housing vouchers show causal income gains for children who moved] — Chyn, E. (2018), "Moved to Opportunity," American Economic Review, 108(10), 3028–3056. [public housing demolition as natural experiment; improved long-run outcomes for displaced children]

Theoretical foundations: Wilson, W.J. (1987), The Truly Disadvantaged. — Sampson, R. (2012), Great American City: Chicago and the Enduring Neighborhood Effect.
Economic stability multipliers
Income volatility as distinct from income level; consumption shocks and child outcomes
Descriptive / mechanistic: Morduch, J. & Schneider, R. (2017), The Financial Diaries: How American Families Cope in a World of Uncertainty. — Duncan, G. & Brooks-Gunn, J. (1997), Consequences of Growing Up Poor.

[de-identified bank account data; spending drops sharply at predictable income decline — causal evidence that income shocks drive consumption instability independently of baseline income level]

First-generation navigation, information access, and college opportunity
Theoretical / mechanistic: Putnam, R. (2015), Our Kids: The American Dream in Crisis. — Jack, A. (2019), The Privileged Poor: How Elite Colleges Are Failing Disadvantaged Students. — Lareau, A. (2003), Unequal Childhoods: Class, Race, and Family Life.

Limitations
What this model does not capture
Disability status, sexual orientation and gender identity beyond the binary, undocumented immigration status, regional variation within the US, specific state-level policy environments, the role of individual contingency (a teacher, a program, a moment of luck), and the interaction effects between variables beyond what the multiplicative model approximates. The model is a simplification. It is designed to make structural forces legible, not to provide a complete account of any individual life.
