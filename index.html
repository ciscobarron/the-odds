Methodology & Sources

How the odds are calculated.

This tool models the structural sorting system of the United States using a chain of conditional probability multipliers — each one derived from peer-reviewed research on the relationship between demographic starting conditions and life outcomes. The evidence base draws from three tiers: administrative and government data (descriptive baselines), top-tier academic research using quasi-experimental and natural experiment designs (causal estimates), and foundational sociological theory (mechanisms). Where sources are descriptive rather than causal, this is noted. The model does not claim to be a complete account of any individual life. It claims to make visible what the data consistently show: that starting conditions are among the strongest predictors of where people land, and that those conditions are not equally shared.

```
The model
Structural multiplier approach
Each demographic input is assigned a multiplier based on the relative probability of achieving above-median educational and economic outcomes given that starting condition. Multipliers compound across inputs. The result is a structural difficulty score that is then converted to a percentile using a log scale anchored at observed minimum and maximum values across the input space. The birth cohort size is provided as context but is not part of the structural score.
Percentile calibration
The 50th percentile is anchored at a structural multiplier of approximately 6 — representing a white or Asian-American male born to one immigrant parent with a high school diploma, in a working-class neighborhood with some economic instability, who completed a bachelor's degree with limited institutional guidance. This approximates the median structural profile of the US adult population given observed demographic distributions.
Sources by variable
Race & ethnicity multipliers
Racial wealth gap, educational attainment, incarceration disparities, and hiring discrimination
Descriptive: Bhutta et al. (2020), "Disparities in Wealth by Race and Ethnicity in the 2019 Survey of Consumer Finances," Federal Reserve Board. — NCES (2022), "Status and Trends in the Education of Racial and Ethnic Groups." — Carson (2020), "Prisoners in 2019," Bureau of Justice Statistics. — Pew Research Center (2016), "On Views of Race and Inequality" [note: this source documents perceptions of inequality, not structural inequality itself].

Causal: Kline, P., Rose, E.K., & Walters, C.R. (2022), "Systemic Discrimination Among Large U.S. Employers," Quarterly Journal of Economics, 137(4), 1963–2036. [83,000 fictitious résumés; Black names received 2.1 percentage point lower callback rates; firm-level discrimination identified causally] — Bohren, J.A., Hull, P., & Imas, A. (2025), "Systemic Discrimination: Theory and Measurement," Quarterly Journal of Economics, 140(3), 1743–1799. [iterated audit design showing how direct discrimination compounds across hiring rounds]
Gender multipliers
Wage gap, occupational segregation, caregiving penalties, dynamic discrimination
Descriptive: BLS (2023), "Highlights of Women's Earnings in 2022." — Goldin, C. (2014), "A Grand Gender Convergence: Its Last Chapter," American Economic Review. [note: Goldin documents why convergence slows, not raw gap size — still directionally supports the multiplier].

Causal: Correll, S., Benard, S., & Paik, I. (2007), "Getting a Job: Is There a Motherhood Penalty?" American Journal of Sociology. [audit experiment; mothers penalized relative to non-mothers and men] — Bohren, J.A., Imas, A., & Rosenberg, M. (2019), "The Dynamics of Discrimination: Theory and Evidence," American Economic Review, 109(10), 3395–3436. [field experiment showing how initial gender bias compounds over evaluation history]
Parental education multipliers
Intergenerational transmission of educational attainment and economic mobility
Descriptive: Torche, F. (2011), "Is a College Degree Still the Great Equalizer?" American Journal of Sociology. — NCES (2019), "First-Generation Students: College Access, Persistence, and Postbachelor's Outcomes." — Pew Research Center (2015), "The American Middle Class Is Losing Ground."

Causal: Chetty, R., Friedman, J., Saez, E., Turner, N., & Yagan, D. (2020), "Income Segregation and Intergenerational Mobility Across Colleges in the United States," Quarterly Journal of Economics. [administrative tax data; parental income rank strongly predicts children's college attendance and adult earnings, capturing both education and class transmission]
Immigrant parent multipliers
Immigrant generation effects on education, income, and institutional navigation
Descriptive: Portes, A. & Rumbaut, R. (2001), Legacies: The Story of the Immigrant Second Generation. — NCES (2019), data on first-generation and language-minority students.

Causal context: Chetty, R. et al. (2017), "The Fading American Dream: Trends in Absolute Income Mobility Since 1940," Science, 356(6336), 398–406. [note: this establishes baseline mobility trends for all Americans, not immigrant-specific effects — used here as mobility context] — Abramitzky, R. & Boustan, L. (2022), Streets of Gold: America's Untold Story of Immigrant Success. [historical and quasi-experimental evidence on immigrant mobility patterns across generations]
Neighborhood multipliers
Place-based causal effects on mobility and opportunity — strongest causal domain in the model
Causal: Chetty, R. & Hendren, N. (2018), "The Impacts of Neighborhoods on Intergenerational Mobility I & II," Quarterly Journal of Economics, 133(3). [quasi-experimental; childhood exposure effects on adult income causally estimated] — Chetty, R., Hendren, N., & Katz, L. (2016), "The Effects of Exposure to Better Neighborhoods on Children," American Economic Review. [Moving to Opportunity RCT; randomized housing vouchers show causal income gains for children who moved] — Chyn, E. (2018), "Moved to Opportunity," American Economic Review, 108(10), 3028–3056. [public housing demolition as natural experiment; improved long-run outcomes for displaced children]

Theoretical foundations: Wilson, W.J. (1987), The Truly Disadvantaged. — Sampson, R. (2012), Great American City: Chicago and the Enduring Neighborhood Effect.
Economic stability multipliers
Income volatility as distinct from income level; consumption shocks and child outcomes
Descriptive / mechanistic: Morduch, J. & Schneider, R. (2017), The Financial Diaries: How American Families Cope in a World of Uncertainty. — Duncan, G. & Brooks-Gunn, J. (1997), Consequences of Growing Up Poor.

Causal: Ganong, P. & Noel, P. (2019), "Consumer Spending during Unemployment: Positive and Normative Implications," American Economic Review, 109(7), 2383–2424. [de-identified bank account data; spending drops sharply at predictable income decline — causal evidence that income shocks drive consumption instability independently of baseline income level]
Educational attainment multipliers
Returns to education by credential level
Descriptive: BLS (2023), "Education Pays," Occupational Outlook Handbook. — Hout, M. (2012), "Social and Economic Returns to College Education in the United States," Annual Review of Sociology.

Causal: Autor, D. (2014), "Skills, Education, and the Rise of Earnings Inequality Among the 'Other 99 Percent,'" Science. [documents skill-biased technological change driving credential premiums; partially causal via structural labor market analysis]
Institutional knowledge / social capital multipliers
First-generation navigation, information access, and college opportunity
Theoretical / mechanistic: Putnam, R. (2015), Our Kids: The American Dream in Crisis. — Jack, A. (2019), The Privileged Poor: How Elite Colleges Are Failing Disadvantaged Students. — Lareau, A. (2003), Unequal Childhoods: Class, Race, and Family Life.

Causal: Hoxby, C. & Turner, S. (2015), "What High-Achieving Low-Income Students Know about College," American Economic Review, 105(5), 514–517. [RCT; personalized college information intervention significantly raised low-income students' applications to, enrollment in, and persistence at selective colleges — information access is causally consequential]
Limitations
What this model does not capture
Disability status, sexual orientation and gender identity beyond the binary, undocumented immigration status, regional variation within the US, specific state-level policy environments, the role of individual contingency (a teacher, a program, a moment of luck), and the interaction effects between variables beyond what the multiplicative model approximates. The model is a simplification. It is designed to make structural forces legible, not to provide a complete account of any individual life.
```
